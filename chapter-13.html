✅ Must-know rules and formulas

13.1 Graphing Linear Equations

  • A linear equation’s graph is a line; every point on it is a solution. Make a table of values, plot, connect.

13.2 Slope of a Line

  • Slope m = (y₂ − y₁)/(x₂ − x₁). Positive rises, negative falls, 0 horizontal, undefined vertical.

Extension 13.2 Slopes of Parallel and Perpendicular Lines

  • Parallel lines have the same slope; perpendicular slopes are negative reciprocals (2 and −1/2).

13.3 Graphing Proportional Relationships

  • Proportional: y = mx, line through the origin; slope = unit rate.

13.4 Graphing Linear Equations in Slope-Intercept Form

  • Slope-intercept form: y = mx + b (b = y-intercept). x-intercept: set y = 0.

13.5 Graphing Linear Equations in Standard Form

  • Standard form: ax + by = c. Find intercepts by setting x = 0, then y = 0, or solve for y.

13.6 Writing Equations in Slope-Intercept Form

  • Write y = mx + b from the slope and y-intercept (read from a graph or two points).

13.7 Writing Equations in Point-Slope Form

  • Point-slope form: y − y₁ = m(x − x₁).

⚠️ Common mistakes and how to avoid them
  • Mistake: Subtracting coordinates in different orders on top and bottom.
    Avoid it: If you start with y₂ on top, start with x₂ on the bottom.
  • Mistake: Reading b as the x-intercept.
    Avoid it: b is where the line crosses the y-axis (x = 0).
  • Mistake: Sign errors in point-slope form.
    Avoid it: y − (−3) becomes y + 3.
  • Mistake: Mixing up zero slope and undefined slope.
    Avoid it: Horizontal = 0; vertical = undefined.
  • Mistake: Forgetting to distribute m in point-slope form.
    Avoid it: Multiply m by both x and −x₁.

13.1 Graphing Linear Equations

💡 The big idea

A linear equation, like y = 2x + 1, makes a straight line when you graph it. A solution of the equation is an ordered pair (x, y) that makes it true, and every point on the line is a solution. To graph one, make a table: pick a few x-values, find each y-value, plot those points, and connect them with a straight line. Equations like y = 4 make horizontal lines and equations like x = −2 make vertical lines.

📝 Key vocabulary

  • linear equation: an equation that makes a straight-line graph
  • solution of a linear equation: an ordered pair that makes the equation true
  • ordered pair: a point written (x, y)

✏️ Worked example

Problem: Make a table for y = 2x + 1 using x = −1, 0, and 2.

  1. x = −1: y = 2(−1) + 1 = −1
  2. x = 0: y = 1
  3. x = 2: y = 5

Answer: Points (−1, −1), (0, 1), and (2, 5); draw a line through them.

    13.2 Slope of a Line

    💡 The big idea

    The slope of a line measures its steepness: slope = rise ÷ run = (y₂ − y₁) ÷ (x₂ − x₁). A line that rises from left to right has a positive slope, and one that falls has a negative slope. A horizontal line has slope 0, and a vertical line has an undefined slope because you would divide by zero. The slope is the same no matter which two points on the line you choose.

    📝 Key vocabulary

    • slope: rise over run
    • rise: change in y
    • run: change in x
    • undefined slope: the slope of a vertical line

    ✏️ Worked example

    Problem: Find the slope of the line through (−2, 3) and (4, −6).

    1. Rise: −6 − 3 = −9.
    2. Run: 4 − (−2) = 6.
    3. Slope = −9/6 = −3/2

    Answer: −3/2

      Extension 13.2 Slopes of Parallel and Perpendicular Lines

      💡 The big idea

      Parallel lines are lines in the same plane that never meet, and nonvertical parallel lines have the same slope. Perpendicular lines cross at a right angle. Their slopes are negative reciprocals, which means you flip the fraction and change the sign; their product is −1. For example, a line with slope 2/3 is perpendicular to a line with slope −3/2.

      📝 Key vocabulary

      • parallel lines: lines that never intersect; same slope
      • perpendicular lines: lines that meet at a right angle
      • negative reciprocal: flip the fraction and change the sign

      ✏️ Worked example

      Problem: Line a has slope 4. What slope would a parallel line have? A perpendicular line?

      1. Parallel: same slope, 4.
      2. Perpendicular: negative reciprocal of 4 is −1/4.
      3. Check: 4 × (−1/4) = −1 ✓

      Answer: Parallel: 4; perpendicular: −1/4

        13.3 Graphing Proportional Relationships

        💡 The big idea

        A proportional relationship can be written as y = mx, where m is the slope and also the unit rate. Its graph is a straight line that passes through the origin. You can compare two proportional relationships by comparing their slopes, even if one is shown as a graph and the other as an equation or table. The steeper line has the greater unit rate.

        📝 Key vocabulary

        • proportional relationship: y = mx; a line through the origin
        • unit rate: the amount per 1 unit, equal to the slope m

        ✏️ Worked example

        Problem: Sam earns $42 for 6 hours. Write an equation and find his pay for 10 hours.

        1. m = 42 ÷ 6 = 7, so y = 7x.
        2. y = 7 × 10 = 70

        Answer: y = 7x; $70

          13.4 Graphing Linear Equations in Slope-Intercept Form

          💡 The big idea

          An equation in slope-intercept form looks like y = mx + b. The number m is the slope, and b is the y-intercept, the y-value where the line crosses the y-axis. To graph it, plot the point (0, b), then count the rise and run from there to land on a second point, and connect the two. The x-intercept is where the line crosses the x-axis, which you can find by setting y = 0.

          📝 Key vocabulary

          • slope-intercept form: y = mx + b
          • y-intercept: the y-coordinate where a line crosses the y-axis
          • x-intercept: the x-coordinate where a line crosses the x-axis

          ✏️ Worked example

          Problem: Identify the slope and y-intercept of y = −2x + 6, then find the x-intercept.

          1. m = −2 and b = 6.
          2. x-intercept: 0 = −2x + 6, so x = 3.

          Answer: Slope −2, y-intercept 6, x-intercept 3

            13.5 Graphing Linear Equations in Standard Form

            💡 The big idea

            Standard form writes a line as ax + by = c (a and b can't both be 0). One fast way to graph it is to find both intercepts: let x = 0 to find the y-intercept, and let y = 0 to find the x-intercept, then draw a line through those two points. You can also solve for y to rewrite it in slope-intercept form. Standard form is handy for problems with two kinds of items, like adult and child tickets.

            📝 Key vocabulary

            • standard form: ax + by = c
            • intercepts: the points where a line crosses the axes

            ✏️ Worked example

            Problem: Find the intercepts of 3x + 4y = 12.

            1. x = 0: 4y = 12, so y = 3 → (0, 3).
            2. y = 0: 3x = 12, so x = 4 → (4, 0).

            Answer: (0, 3) and (4, 0)

              13.6 Writing Equations in Slope-Intercept Form

              💡 The big idea

              To write an equation in slope-intercept form, y = mx + b, you need the slope m and the y-intercept b. From a graph, read the y-intercept where the line crosses the y-axis and find the slope from two points. From two points, find the slope first, then plug in one point to solve for b. In real-life problems, the slope is often a rate, and the y-intercept is a starting amount.

              📝 Key vocabulary

              • slope-intercept form: y = mx + b
              • initial value: the starting amount, the y-intercept in a real-life problem

              ✏️ Worked example

              Problem: Write an equation of the line through (0, −4) and (2, 2).

              1. The point (0, −4) sits on the y-axis, so b = −4.
              2. m = (2 − (−4)) ÷ (2 − 0) = 6 ÷ 2 = 3.
              3. y = 3x − 4

              Answer: y = 3x − 4

                13.7 Writing Equations in Point-Slope Form

                💡 The big idea

                When you know the slope m and any point (x₁, y₁) on a line, you can use point-slope form: y − y₁ = m(x − x₁). This is useful when you do not know the y-intercept. Plug in the slope and the point, and then you can simplify to slope-intercept form if you want. If you are given two points, find the slope first and then use either point.

                📝 Key vocabulary

                • point-slope form: y − y₁ = m(x − x₁)
                • (x₁, y₁): a known point on the line

                ✏️ Worked example

                Problem: Write an equation of the line through (2, 5) with slope 3, then rewrite it in slope-intercept form.

                1. Point-slope: y − 5 = 3(x − 2).
                2. Distribute: y − 5 = 3x − 6.
                3. Add 5: y = 3x − 1.

                Answer: y − 5 = 3(x − 2), or y = 3x − 1
