🌋 Unit B: Geological Processes

Earth’s layers, moving plates, earthquakes and volcanoes, the rock cycle, Earth’s resources, and how to judge the geological risk of a site.

Book: Issues and Science for California, Grade 7 (SEPUP, 3rd edition, NGSS), Unit B (pages B-3 to B-115, 18 activities).

🏆 Revision Plan: Unit B

Test coming up? Use this plan the night before, then check the must-know facts and common misconceptions.

⏰ Night-before plan
  1. Sketch Earth’s layers in order and label them (crust, mantle, outer core, inner core, lithosphere, asthenosphere).
  2. Draw the three plate boundaries with arrows and one example of each.
  3. List four pieces of evidence for plate tectonics.
  4. Draw the rock cycle from memory.
  5. Take one practice test and review the explanations.

✅ Must-know facts
  • Earthquakes and volcanoes cluster along plate boundaries (Ring of Fire).
  • Divergent = apart (ridges); convergent = together (trenches, mountains, volcanoes); transform = slide past (San Andreas).
  • Plates move a few cm per year, driven by mantle convection.
  • Seafloor is youngest at mid-ocean ridges.
  • Igneous (cooling), sedimentary (compaction/cementation), metamorphic (heat/pressure).
  • Permeable materials (gravel, sand) let water flow; clay doesn’t.
  • Most mineral resources are nonrenewable.

⚠️ Common misconceptions
  • Myth: The mantle is liquid lava.
    Actually: The mantle is mostly solid rock that flows very slowly.
  • Myth: Earthquakes happen randomly anywhere.
    Actually: Most happen along plate boundaries.
  • Myth: Continents float on the ocean.
    Actually: Plates (with continents and ocean floor) move on the asthenosphere.
  • Myth: Rocks never change.
    Actually: They are always changing through the rock cycle, just slowly.
  • Myth: Scientists can predict exactly when an earthquake will hit.
    Actually: They estimate probabilities only.

💯 Test tips
  • When a question mentions a trench or volcano chain, think subduction (convergent).
  • For rate questions, multiply rate × time and watch the units (cm vs km).
  • Name the evidence in short answers; “because of plates” alone is not enough.
  • For site-risk questions, list the hazards (earthquake, volcano, landslide, groundwater) before choosing.
  • Scenario questions describe a new situation: name the science idea it is really about before you answer.
  • For short answers, write a full sentence that uses the key vocabulary word and gives a reason (“because …”).

B1 Choosing a Safe Site: Groundwater and Landslides

Book: Activities 1, 2, 3 (Storing Nuclear Waste, Investigating Groundwater, Modeling Landslides)

💡 Summary

The unit starts with a real-world question: where could dangerous, long-lasting waste such as nuclear waste be stored safely for thousands of years? A good site must stay stable and dry, so geologists study groundwater, the water that soaks into the ground and fills spaces between soil and rock particles. Materials with large, connected spaces (like gravel and sand) are porous and permeable and let water move through quickly, while clay and solid rock slow it down. Landslides, the rapid downhill movement of rock and soil, are more likely on steep, wet, unvegetated slopes and are one of the hazards a site must avoid.

📝 Key vocabulary

  • groundwater: water that soaks into the ground and fills the spaces in soil and rock
  • porosity: how much open space a material has to hold water
  • permeability: how easily water can flow through a material
  • aquifer: a layer of rock or sediment that holds and moves groundwater
  • landslide: a sudden downhill movement of soil and rock
  • hazard: a natural event that can harm people or property

🔑 Key concepts

  • Waste storage sites need stable rock, low earthquake and volcano risk, and little moving groundwater.
  • Gravel and sand are permeable; clay and unbroken rock are much less permeable.
  • Groundwater could carry leaking waste to wells, rivers and people.
  • Steep slopes, heavy rain and loss of plant roots raise landslide risk.
  • Scientists weigh many factors and trade-offs when choosing a site.

B2 Earthquakes and Volcanoes

Book: Activities 4, 5, 6, 9, 18 (Natural Hazards Caused by Earthquakes and Volcanoes, Modeling Volcanic Eruptions, Mapping Locations of Earthquakes and Volcanoes, Modeling Earthquakes, Evaluating Site Risk)

💡 Summary

Earthquakes happen when rocks break and slip along faults, releasing energy as seismic waves that shake the ground and can cause landslides, building collapse and tsunamis. Volcanoes form where molten rock (magma) rises and erupts as lava, ash and gas; thick, gassy magma erupts explosively, while runny magma flows more gently. When earthquakes and volcanoes are plotted on a world map, most fall in narrow belts, such as the Ring of Fire around the Pacific Ocean, which follow plate boundaries. Scientists use these patterns, hazard maps and past events to judge risk, though they cannot predict the exact time of an earthquake.

📝 Key vocabulary

  • earthquake: shaking of the ground caused by rocks suddenly moving along a fault
  • fault: a break in Earth’s crust where rocks move
  • epicenter: the point on the surface directly above where an earthquake starts
  • magma / lava: molten rock below the surface / molten rock that reaches the surface
  • tsunami: a series of huge ocean waves often caused by an undersea earthquake
  • Ring of Fire: the belt of frequent earthquakes and volcanoes around the Pacific Ocean

🔑 Key concepts

  • Earthquakes and volcanoes cluster along plate boundaries, not randomly.
  • Thick (viscous), gas-rich magma causes explosive eruptions; thin magma makes lava flows.
  • Hazards: ground shaking, landslides, tsunamis, lava, ash, toxic gases.
  • Engineers reduce damage with flexible, reinforced buildings and early warning systems.
  • Risk depends on both the hazard and how many people and buildings are exposed.

B3 Earth’s Moving Surface and Inner Layers

Book: Activities 7, 8 (Observing Earth’s Moving Surface, Beneath Earth’s Surface)

💡 Summary

Earth’s surface is slowly moving: GPS stations show the ground shifting a few centimeters each year, about as fast as fingernails grow. Underneath, Earth has layers: a thin, rocky crust; a thick mantle of hot rock that can flow very slowly; a liquid outer core; and a solid inner core made mostly of iron and nickel. The crust plus the rigid top of the mantle form the lithosphere, which is broken into plates that ride on the softer asthenosphere below. Scientists learned about the layers mostly from how seismic waves bend and change speed as they travel through Earth.

📝 Key vocabulary

  • crust: Earth’s thin, rocky outer layer
  • mantle: the thick layer of hot rock under the crust
  • outer core / inner core: liquid iron-nickel layer / solid iron-nickel center
  • lithosphere: the rigid outer shell: crust plus uppermost mantle, broken into plates
  • asthenosphere: the soft, slowly flowing part of the upper mantle under the lithosphere
  • GPS: satellite system that measures precise positions, used to track plate motion

🔑 Key concepts

  • Layers from outside in: crust → mantle → outer core (liquid) → inner core (solid).
  • Temperature and pressure increase with depth.
  • Plates move a few centimeters per year.
  • Seismic waves reveal Earth’s interior because they change speed and direction in different materials.
  • Oceanic crust is thinner and denser than continental crust.

B4 Plate Tectonics

Book: Activities 10, 11, 12, 13, 14 (Plate Boundaries, Understanding Plate Boundaries, The Continent Puzzle, The Theory of Plate Tectonics, What Makes the Plates Move?)

💡 Summary

The theory of plate tectonics says the lithosphere is broken into large plates that move slowly over the asthenosphere. Evidence includes continents whose coastlines fit like puzzle pieces, matching fossils and rock layers on continents now separated by oceans, and the pattern of earthquakes, volcanoes and seafloor ages. At divergent boundaries plates pull apart and new crust forms (mid-ocean ridges); at convergent boundaries plates push together, forming trenches, volcanoes and mountains; at transform boundaries plates slide past each other, like the San Andreas Fault. Plates are moved mainly by convection in the mantle, where hot rock slowly rises and cooler rock sinks, along with the pull of sinking plate edges.

📝 Key vocabulary

  • plate tectonics: the theory that Earth’s lithosphere is made of moving plates
  • divergent boundary: where plates move apart and new crust forms
  • convergent boundary: where plates move toward each other; one may sink (subduction)
  • transform boundary: where plates slide past each other sideways
  • subduction: when a denser plate sinks under another into the mantle
  • convection current: circular movement in a fluid caused by heating and cooling
  • Pangaea: the supercontinent that existed about 300–200 million years ago

🔑 Key concepts

  • Evidence: coastline fit, matching fossils (e.g. Mesosaurus, Glossopteris), matching rocks, seafloor spreading and earthquake patterns.
  • Divergent → ridges/rift valleys; convergent → trenches, volcanoes, mountains; transform → earthquakes along faults.
  • Seafloor is youngest at mid-ocean ridges and gets older farther away.
  • Mantle convection and slab pull drive plate motion.
  • California’s San Andreas Fault is a transform boundary between the Pacific and North American plates.

B5 The Rock Cycle and Earth’s Resources

Book: Activities 15, 16, 17 (The Rock Cycle, Rocks as a Resource, Enough Resources for All?)

💡 Summary

Rocks are always slowly changing in the rock cycle, powered by Earth’s internal heat and by the sun-driven water cycle. Igneous rock forms when magma or lava cools; weathering and erosion break rocks into sediment that is deposited, pressed and cemented into sedimentary rock; and heat and pressure deep underground change rocks into metamorphic rock. People rely on rocks and minerals for building materials, metals and energy, but these resources are unevenly distributed and form over millions of years, so they are nonrenewable on human time scales. Using them responsibly means reducing, reusing and recycling and weighing the impacts of mining.

📝 Key vocabulary

  • igneous rock: rock formed when molten rock cools and hardens
  • sedimentary rock: rock formed when sediments are compacted and cemented
  • metamorphic rock: rock changed by heat and pressure without melting
  • weathering / erosion: breaking rock into pieces / moving those pieces by water, wind or ice
  • mineral: a naturally formed, inorganic solid with a definite structure
  • nonrenewable resource: a resource that forms far more slowly than people use it

🔑 Key concepts

  • Any rock type can become any other type through the rock cycle.
  • Cooling fast (lava) makes small crystals; cooling slowly (magma) makes large crystals.
  • Fossils are usually found in sedimentary rock.
  • Mineral resources are unevenly distributed because of past geologic processes.
  • Recycling metals saves energy and conserves resources.
