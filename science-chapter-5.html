How engineers design devices that help the human body: the engineering design process, body systems and replacement parts, artificial bones and heart valves, criteria, constraints and trade-offs, and biomechanics.

🏆 Revision Plan: Unit E

Test coming up? Use this plan the night before, then check the must-know facts and common misconceptions.

⏰ Night-before plan
  1. Draw the engineering design cycle from memory.
  2. Write the difference between criteria and constraints with an example of each.
  3. List levels of organization cell → organism.
  4. Practice one weighted decision matrix calculation.
  5. Explain how muscles, tendons and joints move a finger. Then take a practice test.

✅ Must-know facts
  • Engineers design solutions; scientists explain the natural world.
  • Criteria = what it must do; constraints = limits (cost, time, materials, safety).
  • Prototype → test → analyze data → improve (iterate).
  • Cell → tissue → organ → organ system → organism.
  • Implants must be strong, durable and biocompatible.
  • Heart valves allow one-way flow.
  • Muscles pull in pairs; tendons connect muscle to bone; friction helps grip.

⚠️ Common misconceptions
  • Myth: Engineers just build things; they don’t use science.
    Actually: They apply science and math and test with data.
  • Myth: A failed test means the design is bad.
    Actually: Failures provide data to improve the design.
  • Myth: Muscles can push bones.
    Actually: Muscles only pull; opposing pairs move joints both ways.
  • Myth: The best design is the one with the highest score on one criterion.
    Actually: Look at all criteria and their weights.

💯 Test tips
  • When asked for criteria vs constraints, check: is it a goal (criterion) or a limit (constraint)?
  • Show the multiplication when a criterion is weighted.
  • Tie each design choice to a criterion it helps meet.
  • Use “because” to justify engineering decisions with evidence.
  • Scenario questions describe a new situation: name the science idea it is really about before you answer.
  • For short answers, write a full sentence that uses the key vocabulary word and gives a reason (“because …”).

E1 What Engineers Do

Book: Activities 1, 2, 6 (Save Fred!, Me, an Engineer?, The Work of an Engineer)

💡 Summary

Engineers use science and math to design solutions to human problems, and biomedical engineers focus on health and the human body. The engineering design process starts by clearly defining the problem, including criteria and constraints, then brainstorming many ideas, choosing a promising one, building and testing a prototype, and improving it. Teamwork and communication matter: engineers share data, listen to the people who will use the design, and explain why they chose one solution over others. A simple challenge like rescuing a gummy “Fred” with limited tools shows how planning and testing beat guessing.

📝 Key vocabulary

  • engineer: a person who designs solutions to problems using science and math
  • biomedical engineering: engineering that designs devices and treatments for the body and health
  • engineering design process: the steps of defining, brainstorming, building, testing and improving a solution
  • criteria: what a successful solution must do
  • constraints: limits on a solution, like cost, materials and time

🔑 Key concepts

  • Define the problem first: who needs it, criteria, constraints.
  • Brainstorm many ideas before choosing one.
  • Test, then use data to improve (iterate).
  • Scientists ask questions about nature; engineers design solutions to problems.

E2 Body Systems and Bionic Replacements

Book: Activity 3 (Bionic Bodies)

💡 Summary

The body is made of cells, which form tissues, which form organs, which work together in organ systems such as the skeletal, muscular, circulatory, respiratory and nervous systems. Because systems depend on each other, a problem in one organ can affect the whole body. Biomedical engineers design replacements and supports, such as artificial joints, prosthetic limbs, pacemakers, cochlear implants and heart valves, that copy the function of the original part. A good replacement must do the job, be safe inside the body (biocompatible) and last a long time.

📝 Key vocabulary

  • cell: the basic unit of life
  • tissue: a group of similar cells working together
  • organ: a structure of different tissues that does a specific job
  • organ system: organs working together to perform a major function
  • prosthesis: an artificial device that replaces a missing body part
  • biocompatible: safe to place in the body without harmful reactions

🔑 Key concepts

  • Levels of organization: cell → tissue → organ → organ system → organism.
  • Body systems interact (muscles move bones; the heart pumps blood carrying oxygen from the lungs).
  • Replacement parts must match the function of the original.
  • Materials inside the body must be biocompatible.

E3 Artificial Bone and Heart Valve Models

Book: Activities 4, 5 (Artificial Bone Model, Artificial Heart Valve)

💡 Summary

Bones must be strong to support weight yet light enough to move, and their structure, a hard outer layer with a spongy, honeycomb-like inside, gives both strength and lightness. In an artificial bone model, students test how materials and shapes (solid vs hollow tubes, different fillings) hold up under a load. Heart valves are flaps that let blood flow one way through the heart and close to stop backflow, so an artificial valve must open easily, seal tightly and work billions of times. Modeling and testing let engineers compare designs safely before using them in people.

📝 Key vocabulary

  • load: the force or weight a structure must support
  • spongy bone: the lightweight, honeycomb-like tissue inside bones
  • valve: a flap that lets fluid flow in only one direction
  • backflow: fluid flowing the wrong way
  • model: a representation used to test or explain something

🔑 Key concepts

  • Hollow tubes can be strong and light, like real bones.
  • Test designs with the same load for a fair comparison.
  • Heart valves keep blood flowing one direction.
  • A good valve opens easily, seals tightly and is durable.
  • Models let engineers test safely, but have limitations.

E4 Criteria, Constraints and Trade-offs

Book: Activity 7 (Snack Bar) and design decisions across the unit

💡 Summary

Real designs must balance many needs. In a design such as a nutritious snack bar for a specific group of people, criteria might include calories, protein, taste and shelf life, while constraints include cost, allergies and available ingredients. Engineers use a decision matrix, scoring each option on each criterion, sometimes giving important criteria extra weight, to compare designs fairly. Improving one feature often makes another worse, and recognizing these trade-offs and explaining choices with evidence is a core engineering skill.

📝 Key vocabulary

  • trade-off: giving up some of one quality to gain another
  • decision matrix: a table that scores design options against criteria
  • weighting: counting some criteria more than others because they matter more
  • optimize: make a design as good as possible within the constraints
  • nutrient: a substance in food the body needs, such as protein or carbohydrates

🔑 Key concepts

  • List criteria and constraints before comparing options.
  • A decision matrix makes comparisons fair and visible.
  • Weighted criteria count more toward the total score.
  • Trade-offs are normal; justify the final choice with evidence.

E5 Biomechanics and Grip

Book: Activities 8, 9 (Investigating Biomechanics, Get a Grip)

💡 Summary

Biomechanics applies the science of forces and motion to the body. Bones act like levers, joints act like pivots, and muscles pull on bones through tendons to produce movement, and since muscles can only pull, they work in pairs, like the biceps and triceps. The human hand uses many joints and an opposable thumb to grip objects of different sizes, applying force and using friction to hold on. Designing a prosthetic or robotic hand means copying these functions: the right number of moving parts, a way to close and open, and gripping surfaces with enough friction.

📝 Key vocabulary

  • biomechanics: the study of forces and movement in living things
  • joint: a place where two bones meet and can move
  • tendon: tissue that connects a muscle to a bone
  • opposable thumb: a thumb that can touch the other fingertips, allowing a strong grip
  • friction: a force that resists sliding between surfaces
  • lever: a rigid bar that turns around a pivot point

🔑 Key concepts

  • Muscles only pull; they work in opposing pairs.
  • Bones act as levers and joints as pivots.
  • Tendons connect muscle to bone; ligaments connect bone to bone.
  • Grip depends on force and friction.
  • Engineers copy body structures in prosthetic and robotic hands.
