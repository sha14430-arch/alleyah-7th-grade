✅ Must-know rules and formulas

7.1 Adjacent and Vertical Angles

  • Adjacent angles share a vertex and a side. Vertical angles (opposite at an X) are congruent.

7.2 Complementary and Supplementary Angles

  • Complementary angles add to 90°; supplementary angles add to 180°.

7.3 Triangles

  • Classify triangles by angles (acute, right, obtuse) and by sides (equilateral, isosceles, scalene).
  • Triangle Inequality: the two shorter sides must add to MORE than the longest side.

Extension 7.3 Angle Measures of Triangles

  • The angles of a triangle add to 180°.

7.4 Quadrilaterals

  • The angles of a quadrilateral add to 360°.
  • Square ⊂ rectangle ⊂ parallelogram; square ⊂ rhombus ⊂ parallelogram. Trapezoid: exactly one pair of parallel sides.

7.5 Scale Drawings

  • Scale drawing: drawing length / actual length = scale. Set up a proportion with matching units.
  • Areas scale by the square of the scale factor.

⚠️ Common mistakes and how to avoid them
  • Mistake: Mixing up complementary (90°) and supplementary (180°).
    Avoid it: Memory trick: C comes before S, and 90 comes before 180.
  • Mistake: Thinking vertical angles add to 180°.
    Avoid it: Vertical angles are EQUAL; adjacent angles on a line add to 180°.
  • Mistake: Forgetting to check the Triangle Inequality with the two SHORTEST sides.
    Avoid it: Add the two smallest and compare with the largest.
  • Mistake: Using 180° for a quadrilateral.
    Avoid it: Quadrilaterals add to 360°.
  • Mistake: Mixed units in scale problems.
    Avoid it: Convert so the units match before setting up the proportion.

💯 Test-taking tips for a perfect score
  • Show every step. Write each step on its own line so you (and your teacher) can find mistakes, and so you can earn partial credit.
  • Check the signs. Before you finish a problem, ask: should the answer be positive or negative? Circle the sign.
  • Estimate first. Round the numbers and get a rough answer. If your final answer is far from your estimate, redo it.
  • Reread word problems. Underline what the question asks for and the important numbers. Make sure you answer the actual question.
  • Check units and form. Include units (feet, $, square inches) and give the answer in the form asked (simplest form, decimal, percent).
  • Do the easy ones first. Skip a hard problem, mark it, and come back. Don’t leave anything blank.
  • Go back and verify. Use extra time to plug answers back in, redo calculations a different way, and check that every question is answered.
  • Stay calm. If you get stuck, take a deep breath, write what you know, and try a small example.

7.1 Adjacent and Vertical Angles

💡 The big idea

Adjacent angles share a vertex and a side but do not overlap, like two slices of pizza next to each other. When two lines cross, the angles directly across from each other are vertical angles, and vertical angles are always congruent (equal in measure). Angles next to each other along a straight line add up to 180°. You can use these facts to write and solve equations for missing angles.

📝 Key vocabulary

  • adjacent angles: angles that share a vertex and a side without overlapping
  • vertical angles: opposite angles formed by two crossing lines
  • congruent angles: angles with the same measure

✏️ Worked example

Problem: Two lines cross. One angle is 65°. Find the vertical angle and an adjacent angle.

  1. The vertical angle is congruent: 65°.
  2. An adjacent angle on the straight line: 180° − 65° = 115°.

Answer: 65° and 115°

    7.2 Complementary and Supplementary Angles

    💡 The big idea

    Two angles are complementary if their measures add up to 90°, like the two parts of a corner of a square. Two angles are supplementary if their measures add up to 180°, like two angles that make a straight line. To find a missing angle, subtract from 90° or 180°. Tip for remembering: "C" comes before "S" in the alphabet, and 90 comes before 180.

    📝 Key vocabulary

    • complementary angles: two angles whose measures add to 90°
    • supplementary angles: two angles whose measures add to 180°

    ✏️ Worked example

    Problem: Angles A and B are supplementary, and angle A is 3 times angle B. Find both angles.

    1. B + 3B = 180, so 4B = 180.
    2. B = 45°, and A = 3 × 45° = 135°.

    Answer: A = 135°, B = 45°

      7.3 Triangles

      💡 The big idea

      Triangles can be named by their angles: acute (all angles less than 90°), right (one 90° angle), or obtuse (one angle more than 90°). They can also be named by their sides: equilateral (3 congruent sides), isosceles (at least 2 congruent sides), or scalene (no congruent sides). You can construct a triangle with a ruler and protractor when you know some of its sides and angles. Not every set of three lengths works: the two shorter sides must add up to more than the longest side.

      📝 Key vocabulary

      • acute / right / obtuse triangle: classified by its largest angle
      • equilateral / isosceles / scalene triangle: classified by how many sides are congruent
      • congruent sides: sides with the same length

      ✏️ Worked example

      Problem: Can you make a triangle with sides 4 cm, 5 cm, and 10 cm?

      1. Add the two shorter sides: 4 + 5 = 9.
      2. 9 is not greater than 10, so the sides cannot meet.

      Answer: No, a triangle is not possible.

        Extension 7.3 Angle Measures of Triangles

        💡 The big idea

        The three interior angles of any triangle always add up to 180°. You can see this by tearing off the corners of a paper triangle and lining them up: they form a straight line. To find a missing angle, subtract the two known angles from 180°. If the angles are written with a variable, add them, set the total equal to 180, and solve.

        📝 Key vocabulary

        • interior angle: an angle inside the triangle at a vertex
        • angle sum of a triangle: always 180°

        ✏️ Worked example

        Problem: A triangle has angles x°, 2x°, and 3x°. Find each angle.

        1. x + 2x + 3x = 180, so 6x = 180.
        2. x = 30, so the angles are 30°, 60°, and 90°.

        Answer: 30°, 60°, and 90°

          7.4 Quadrilaterals

          💡 The big idea

          A quadrilateral is a polygon with 4 sides, and its 4 angles always add up to 360°. Special quadrilaterals include the trapezoid (only one pair of sides parallel), the parallelogram (two pairs of parallel sides), the rectangle (a parallelogram with 4 right angles), the rhombus (a parallelogram with 4 congruent sides), and the square (both a rectangle and a rhombus). A kite has two pairs of congruent sides that are next to each other. To find a missing angle, subtract the other three from 360°.

          📝 Key vocabulary

          • quadrilateral: a four-sided polygon
          • parallelogram: a quadrilateral with two pairs of parallel sides
          • trapezoid: a four-sided shape with just one pair of parallel sides

          ✏️ Worked example

          Problem: A quadrilateral has angles 85°, 110°, and 70°. Find the fourth angle.

          1. 85 + 110 + 70 = 265
          2. 360 − 265 = 95

          Answer: 95°

            7.5 Scale Drawings

            💡 The big idea

            A scale drawing shows a real object larger or smaller than its actual size, like a map or a floor plan. The scale tells how drawing lengths compare to actual lengths, such as 1 in. : 8 ft. To find an actual length, set up a proportion using the scale. A scale factor is a scale without units, like 1 : 50, and it also tells you how much bigger or smaller the drawing is.

            📝 Key vocabulary

            • scale drawing: a proportional drawing of an object
            • scale: the ratio of drawing length to actual length
            • scale factor: a scale with no units

            ✏️ Worked example

            Problem: On a floor plan, the scale is 1 in. : 8 ft. A room is 2.5 in. long on the plan. How long is the real room?

            1. 1/8 = 2.5/x
            2. x = 2.5 × 8 = 20

            Answer: 20 feet
