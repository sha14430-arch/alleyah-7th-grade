✅ Must-know rules and formulas

14.1 Finding Square Roots

  • A square root of n is a number that times itself is n. √49 = 7; both 7 and −7 square to 49.
  • Learn perfect squares 1–225.

14.2 Finding Cube Roots

  • Cube root: ∛27 = 3; ∛(−8) = −2. Learn perfect cubes 1, 8, 27, 64, 125, 216, 343, 512, 729, 1000.

14.3 The Pythagorean Theorem

  • Pythagorean Theorem (right triangles only): a² + b² = c², c = hypotenuse (longest side, across from the right angle).

14.4 Approximating Square Roots

  • Estimate √n between two perfect squares: √50 is between 7 and 8, closer to 7.
  • Irrational numbers (like √2, π) cannot be written as fractions; their decimals never end or repeat.

Extension 14.4 Repeating Decimals

  • Repeating decimal → fraction: one repeating digit → over 9 (0.444… = 4/9); two repeating digits → over 99 (0.272727… = 27/99 = 3/11).

14.5 Using the Pythagorean Theorem

  • Distance between points: make a right triangle with legs = horizontal and vertical change, then use a² + b² = c².
  • Converse: if a² + b² = c², the triangle is right.

⚠️ Common mistakes and how to avoid them
  • Mistake: Adding the leg and hypotenuse squares when finding a leg.
    Avoid it: For a leg: b² = c² − a².
  • Mistake: Forgetting to take the square root at the end.
    Avoid it: c² = 100 means c = 10, not 100.
  • Mistake: Calling every square root irrational.
    Avoid it: √16 = 4 is rational; only non-perfect squares are irrational.
  • Mistake: Thinking a negative has no cube root.
    Avoid it: ∛(−27) = −3.
  • Mistake: Estimating √n to the wrong whole number.
    Avoid it: Compare n to the perfect squares on both sides.

💯 Test-taking tips for a perfect score
  • Show every step. Write each step on its own line so you (and your teacher) can find mistakes, and so you can earn partial credit.
  • Check the signs. Before you finish a problem, ask: should the answer be positive or negative? Circle the sign.
  • Estimate first. Round the numbers and get a rough answer. If your final answer is far from your estimate, redo it.
  • Reread word problems. Underline what the question asks for and the important numbers. Make sure you answer the actual question.
  • Check units and form. Include units (feet, $, square inches) and give the answer in the form asked (simplest form, decimal, percent).
  • Do the easy ones first. Skip a hard problem, mark it, and come back. Don’t leave anything blank.
  • Go back and verify. Use extra time to plug answers back in, redo calculations a different way, and check that every question is answered.
  • Stay calm. If you get stuck, take a deep breath, write what you know, and try a small example.

14.1 Finding Square Roots

💡 The big idea

Taking a square root undoes squaring: you look for the number that, times itself, gives you what you started with. Every positive number has two square roots, one positive and one negative: 5 × 5 = 25 and (−5) × (−5) = 25. The radical sign √ means the positive square root, so √25 = 5, and the number under it is the radicand. Numbers like 1, 4, 9, 16, and 25 are perfect squares because their square roots are whole numbers.

📝 Key vocabulary

  • square root: a number that times itself equals a given number
  • perfect square: a number whose square root is an integer
  • radical sign (√): the symbol for a square root
  • radicand: the number under the radical sign

✏️ Worked example

Problem: Solve x² = 81 and find √(4/49).

  1. x = 9 or x = −9 because both square to 81.
  2. √(4/49) = √4 ÷ √49 = 2/7.

Answer: x = ±9; √(4/49) = 2/7

    14.2 Finding Cube Roots

    💡 The big idea

    A cube root undoes cubing: it is the number you multiply by itself three times to get back the original. For example, 4 × 4 × 4 = 64, so the cube root of 64 is 4, written ∛64 = 4. Numbers like 8, 27, 64, and 125 are perfect cubes. Unlike square roots, negative numbers have real cube roots: ∛(−27) = −3 because (−3) × (−3) × (−3) = −27.

    📝 Key vocabulary

    • cube root: a number that cubed equals a given number
    • perfect cube: a number that is an integer cubed
    • ∛: the cube root symbol

    ✏️ Worked example

    Problem: Solve x³ = −125.

    1. Take the cube root of both sides.
    2. x = ∛(−125) = −5 because (−5)³ = −125.

    Answer: x = −5

      14.3 The Pythagorean Theorem

      💡 The big idea

      In a right triangle, the two sides meeting at the square corner are the legs, and the longest side, across from the right angle, is the hypotenuse. The Pythagorean Theorem says a² + b² = c², where a and b are the legs and c is the hypotenuse. You can use it to find any missing side when you know the other two. The theorem only works for right triangles.

      📝 Key vocabulary

      • legs: the two shorter sides that meet at the right angle
      • hypotenuse: the longest side, opposite the right angle
      • Pythagorean Theorem: a² + b² = c²

      ✏️ Worked example

      Problem: A right triangle has legs 6 cm and 8 cm. Find the hypotenuse.

      1. 6² + 8² = c²
      2. 36 + 64 = 100 = c²
      3. c = √100 = 10

      Answer: 10 cm

        14.4 Approximating Square Roots

        💡 The big idea

        An irrational number cannot be written as a fraction of integers, and its decimal goes on forever without repeating. If a whole number is not a perfect square, its square root (like √2 or √30) is irrational. Rational and irrational numbers together make up the real numbers. To approximate a square root, find the two perfect squares it lies between, then test decimals: √30 is between 5 and 6, and closer to 5.5 because 5.5² = 30.25.

        📝 Key vocabulary

        • irrational number: a number that cannot be written as a ratio of integers
        • real numbers: all rational and irrational numbers
        • approximate: find a value that is close

        ✏️ Worked example

        Problem: Estimate √30 to the nearest tenth.

        1. 25 < 30 < 36, so 5 < √30 < 6.
        2. 5.4² = 29.16 and 5.5² = 30.25.
        3. 30 is closer to 30.25, so √30 ≈ 5.5.

        Answer: √30 ≈ 5.5

          Extension 14.4 Repeating Decimals

          💡 The big idea

          Every repeating decimal is a rational number, so it can be written as a fraction. The trick is to use algebra: let x equal the decimal, multiply by 10, 100, or 1,000 (depending on how many digits repeat), and subtract to make the repeating part disappear. For example, if x = 0.444…, then 10x = 4.444…, so 10x − x = 4 and x = 4/9. This shows that 0.999… is actually equal to 1!

          📝 Key vocabulary

          • repeating decimal: a decimal with a block of digits that repeats forever
          • bar notation: a bar over the repeating digits

          ✏️ Worked example

          Problem: Write 0.272727… as a fraction.

          1. Let x = 0.2727… Two digits repeat, so multiply by 100.
          2. 100x = 27.2727…
          3. 100x − x = 27, so 99x = 27 and x = 27/99 = 3/11.

          Answer: 3/11

            14.5 Using the Pythagorean Theorem

            💡 The big idea

            The converse of the Pythagorean Theorem works backward: if a triangle's sides fit a² + b² = c², the triangle must have a right angle. You can use this to check whether a corner is really square. You can also measure the distance between two points on a coordinate grid by making a right triangle whose legs are the horizontal and vertical gaps. It solves lots of real-life problems, like how far a ramp or a diagonal path stretches.

            📝 Key vocabulary

            • converse: a statement with the "if" and "then" parts switched
            • distance between two points: found with the Pythagorean Theorem

            ✏️ Worked example

            Problem: Find the distance between (1, 2) and (7, 10).

            1. Horizontal leg: 7 − 1 = 6. Vertical leg: 10 − 2 = 8.
            2. d² = 6² + 8² = 100
            3. d = 10

            Answer: 10 units
