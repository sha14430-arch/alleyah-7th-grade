🌿 Unit A: Ecology

How living things interact with each other and with their environment, how matter and energy move through ecosystems, and what happens when a new species is introduced.

Book: Issues and Science for California, Grade 7 (SEPUP, 3rd edition, NGSS), Unit A (pages A-3 to A-101, 19 activities).

✅ Must-know facts
  • Biotic = living; abiotic = nonliving (light, water, temperature, soil, salt).
  • Introduced species can become invasive when they have few predators or diseases.
  • Limiting factors keep populations near carrying capacity.
  • Food web arrows go from food to eater.
  • About 10% of energy moves to the next level of the energy pyramid.
  • Photosynthesis: CO₂ + water + light → sugar + O₂. Respiration: sugar + O₂ → CO₂ + water + energy.
  • Matter cycles; energy flows.

⚠️ Common misconceptions
  • Myth: Plants get their food from the soil.
    Actually: Plants make their food (sugar) from carbon dioxide and water using light; soil gives minerals and water.
  • Myth: Only animals do cellular respiration.
    Actually: Plants respire too, all day and night.
  • Myth: Food web arrows show who eats whom, pointing to the food.
    Actually: Arrows point toward the eater, showing the direction energy moves.
  • Myth: Every introduced species is bad.
    Actually: Only those that spread and cause harm are invasive.
  • Myth: Energy is recycled like matter.
    Actually: Energy is lost as heat at each level and must keep coming in from the sun.

A1 Introduced Species and Ecosystem Change

Book: Activities 1, 2, 18, 19 (The Miracle Fish?, Introduced Species, Too Many Mussels, Presenting the Facts)

💡 Summary

An introduced species is a living thing that people move, on purpose or by accident, into an ecosystem where it did not live before. Some introduced species become invasive: with few predators or diseases to hold them back, they spread quickly and crowd out native species. The unit opens with a fish introduced into a large lake to provide food and income, which raises a real trade-off between benefits for people and harm to the ecosystem. Scientists and communities use evidence to weigh those trade-offs and decide how to manage or prevent introductions.

📝 Key vocabulary

  • ecosystem: all the living things in an area plus the nonliving parts of their environment, interacting together
  • native species: a species that naturally lives in an area
  • introduced species: a species brought into an area by people where it did not live before
  • invasive species: an introduced species that spreads and harms the ecosystem, economy or human health
  • trade-off: giving up one benefit to gain another when making a decision

🔑 Key concepts

  • Not every introduced species is harmful, but invasive ones can change a whole ecosystem.
  • Invasive species often succeed because they lack natural predators, reproduce fast and compete well for food and space.
  • Decisions about species involve trade-offs between economic, social and environmental effects.
  • Preventing an introduction (cleaning boats, not releasing pets) is usually easier and cheaper than removing a species later.

A2 Habitats, Transects and Abiotic Factors

Book: Activities 3, 4, 5, 16 (Data Transects, Taking a Look Outside, A Suitable Habitat, Abiotic Impacts on Ecosystems)

💡 Summary

Every ecosystem has biotic factors (living things) and abiotic factors (nonliving things such as sunlight, water, temperature, soil and salt). A habitat is the place that provides what an organism needs to survive, and each species does best within a certain range of abiotic conditions. Ecologists sample ecosystems with tools like transects, a line along which they record organisms at regular intervals, because counting everything is impossible. Changes in abiotic factors, such as drought or warmer water, can change which organisms can survive in an area.

📝 Key vocabulary

  • biotic factor: a living or once-living part of an ecosystem, such as plants, animals, fungi and bacteria
  • abiotic factor: a nonliving part of an ecosystem, such as light, water, temperature, soil or salinity
  • habitat: the place where an organism lives that supplies its food, water, shelter and space
  • transect: a line through an area along which scientists sample and record organisms
  • sample: a smaller part studied to estimate what the whole area is like

🔑 Key concepts

  • Organisms need food, water, shelter and suitable conditions; a suitable habitat provides these.
  • Each species has a range of tolerance for abiotic factors like temperature or salinity.
  • Transects and quadrats let scientists estimate populations without counting every organism.
  • More samples and careful, repeated measurements make data more reliable.
  • A change in one abiotic factor can ripple through the whole ecosystem.

A3 Populations and Interactions

Book: Activities 6, 12, 13, 17 (Ups and Downs, Population Growth, Interactions in Ecosystems, Effects of an Introduced Species)

💡 Summary

A population is all the members of one species living in an area, and its size goes up and down over time. With plenty of resources a population can grow very quickly, but limiting factors such as food, space, water, predators and disease eventually slow it, so it levels off near the carrying capacity. Organisms also interact: they compete for the same resources, eat or are eaten (predation), or live closely together in symbiosis, which can help both (mutualism), help one without affecting the other (commensalism) or help one while harming the other (parasitism). Predator and prey populations often rise and fall in linked cycles.

📝 Key vocabulary

  • population: all the individuals of one species in an area at the same time
  • limiting factor: a resource or condition that keeps a population from growing larger
  • carrying capacity: the largest population an environment can support over time
  • competition: when organisms need the same limited resource
  • predation: one organism (the predator) hunting and eating another (the prey)
  • mutualism: a relationship in which both species benefit
  • parasitism: a relationship in which one species benefits and the other is harmed

🔑 Key concepts

  • Populations grow fast when resources are plentiful, then level off at the carrying capacity.
  • Limiting factors include food, water, space, shelter, predators and disease.
  • When prey increases, predators that eat it often increase afterwards; then prey falls, and predators fall.
  • Competition is strongest when two species use the same resource in the same place.
  • Symbiosis types: mutualism (+/+), commensalism (+/0), parasitism (+/−).

A4 Food Chains, Food Webs and Energy

Book: Activities 7, 8, 10, 15 (Coughing Up Clues, Eating for Matter and Energy, Energy and Matter in Food, Modeling the Introduction of a New Species)

💡 Summary

Food chains and food webs show who eats whom and in which direction matter and energy move. Producers, like plants and algae, make their own food; consumers eat other organisms; and decomposers break down dead matter and return nutrients to the soil. Scientists can find out what an animal eats from evidence such as owl pellets, the undigested fur and bones an owl coughs up. Only about 10% of the energy at one level of an energy pyramid passes to the next, because most is used for life processes or lost as heat, so there are fewer organisms at the top.

📝 Key vocabulary

  • producer: an organism that makes its own food, usually by photosynthesis
  • consumer: an organism that gets energy by eating other organisms
  • decomposer: an organism such as a fungus or bacterium that breaks down dead material
  • food web: a network of connected food chains in an ecosystem
  • herbivore / carnivore / omnivore: eats plants / eats animals / eats both
  • energy pyramid: a diagram showing energy decreasing at each feeding level

🔑 Key concepts

  • Arrows in a food web point from the food to the eater, the direction energy and matter flow.
  • Energy flows one way through an ecosystem; matter is recycled.
  • About 10% of energy passes to the next level; the rest is used or lost as heat.
  • Removing or adding one species can affect many others in the web.
  • Owl pellets give evidence of diet: bones of voles, shrews and birds show what the owl ate.

A5 Photosynthesis, Cellular Respiration and Cycling of Matter

Book: Activities 9, 11, 14 (Cellular Respiration, A Producer’s Source of Energy, Cycling of Matter)

💡 Summary

Producers use energy from sunlight to turn carbon dioxide and water into sugar (glucose), releasing oxygen; this is photosynthesis and it happens in chloroplasts. Almost all organisms, including plants, then release the energy stored in sugar through cellular respiration, which uses oxygen and gives off carbon dioxide and water; it happens in mitochondria. These two processes connect, so atoms of carbon and oxygen cycle between living things and the air, water and soil. Matter is recycled in ecosystems, while energy flows in from the sun and leaves as heat.

📝 Key vocabulary

  • photosynthesis: the process in which producers use light energy to make sugar from carbon dioxide and water, releasing oxygen
  • cellular respiration: the process in cells that uses oxygen to release energy from sugar, giving off carbon dioxide and water
  • chloroplast: the cell structure in plants and algae where photosynthesis happens
  • mitochondria: cell structures where cellular respiration happens
  • carbon cycle: the movement of carbon atoms between the air, living things, soil and oceans

🔑 Key concepts

  • Photosynthesis: carbon dioxide + water + light energy → sugar + oxygen.
  • Cellular respiration: sugar + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water + energy.
  • Plants do BOTH photosynthesis and cellular respiration.
  • Bromothymol blue (BTB) turns from blue to yellow/green when carbon dioxide is added, a test for respiration.
  • Matter (atoms) cycles; energy flows through and is eventually lost as heat.
