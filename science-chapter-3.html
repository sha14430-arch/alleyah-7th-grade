🏆 Revision Plan: Unit C

Test coming up? Use this plan the night before, then check the must-know facts and common misconceptions.

⏰ Night-before plan
  1. Review element, atom, molecule and compound definitions with one example each.
  2. Practice three density problems (D = m ÷ V) including one with water displacement.
  3. Draw particles for solid, liquid and gas.
  4. Explain polymers and cross-links using a paper-clip chain picture.
  5. Take one practice test and read the explanations.

✅ Must-know facts
  • Elements: one kind of atom; compounds: two or more elements joined.
  • Subscripts count atoms (H₂O = 2 H + 1 O).
  • Physical properties don’t change the substance; chemical properties describe forming new substances.
  • Density = mass ÷ volume; water = 1 g/mL; less dense floats.
  • Adding energy speeds particles: melting, evaporation, boiling.
  • Mass is conserved in changes of state.
  • Polymers = many monomers; cross-links make them stiffer.

⚠️ Common misconceptions
  • Myth: Heavy things sink, light things float.
    Actually: Floating depends on density compared with the liquid.
  • Myth: Particles in a solid don’t move.
    Actually: They vibrate in place.
  • Myth: Melting is a chemical change.
    Actually: It’s a physical change; the substance is the same.
  • Myth: Particles expand when heated.
    Actually: Particles move faster and spread out; they don’t get bigger.
  • Myth: All polymers are plastic.
    Actually: Many are natural: cellulose, proteins, DNA.

💯 Test tips
  • Write the formula D = m ÷ V before plugging numbers in, and include units.
  • For particle questions, describe spacing AND motion.
  • Check “physical vs chemical”: is a new substance formed?
  • Count atoms carefully: a subscript applies only to the symbol right before it.
  • Scenario questions describe a new situation: name the science idea it is really about before you answer.
  • For short answers, write a full sentence that uses the key vocabulary word and gives a reason (“because …”).

C1 Materials and Elements

Book: Activities 1, 2 (Exploring Materials, Investigating Elements)

💡 Summary

Everything around us is made of matter, and engineers choose materials such as metals, glass, wood and plastics by matching their properties to a job. All matter is built from elements, pure substances that cannot be broken down into simpler substances by chemical means; about 90 occur naturally. The periodic table organizes the elements, and elements in the same column (group) have similar properties. Metals are usually shiny, bendable and good conductors of heat and electricity, while nonmetals are often dull, brittle and poor conductors.

📝 Key vocabulary

  • matter: anything that has mass and takes up space
  • element: a pure substance made of only one kind of atom
  • periodic table: a chart that organizes all the elements by their properties
  • metal: an element that is usually shiny, malleable and a good conductor
  • nonmetal: an element that is usually dull, brittle and a poor conductor
  • conductor: a material that lets heat or electricity pass through easily

🔑 Key concepts

  • Each element has a one- or two-letter symbol (O, Fe, Cu, Na).
  • Columns (groups) of the periodic table share similar properties.
  • Metals are on the left and middle; nonmetals on the right.
  • Materials are chosen for a purpose based on their properties.

C2 Physical and Chemical Properties; Density

Book: Activities 3, 4, 5 (Physical and Chemical Properties of Materials, Determining Density, Evaluating Properties of Materials)

💡 Summary

A physical property can be observed or measured without changing what a substance is, such as color, hardness, melting point, solubility and density. A chemical property describes how a substance can change into a new substance, such as whether it burns, rusts or reacts with acid. Density is mass per unit volume (D = m ÷ V), often in g/cm³ or g/mL; it is a characteristic property, the same for any size sample of a pure substance. Objects less dense than water (1 g/mL) float and denser objects sink, and comparing several properties helps identify a material and pick the best one for a design.

📝 Key vocabulary

  • physical property: a trait you can observe without changing the substance (color, density, melting point)
  • chemical property: a trait that describes how a substance reacts to form new substances (flammability, reacting with acid)
  • density: mass per unit volume, D = m ÷ V
  • characteristic property: a property that stays the same no matter how much of the substance you have
  • solubility: how much of a substance dissolves in a liquid

🔑 Key concepts

  • Density = mass ÷ volume; units g/cm³ or g/mL.
  • Water’s density is 1 g/mL; less dense floats, denser sinks.
  • Cutting a sample in half does not change its density.
  • Volume of an irregular object can be found by water displacement.
  • Several properties together identify an unknown substance.

C3 Atoms, Molecules and the Structure of Materials

Book: Activities 6, 7 (Modeling Molecules, Structure and Properties of Materials)

💡 Summary

Atoms are the tiny building blocks of all matter, and each element has its own kind of atom. Atoms join together into molecules, such as water (H₂O: two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom) or carbon dioxide (CO₂), and a chemical formula tells which atoms and how many. Some substances form extended structures instead, like the repeating crystal pattern in salt (NaCl) or diamond. The way atoms are arranged and connected gives a material its properties, which is why graphite and diamond, both pure carbon, are so different.

📝 Key vocabulary

  • atom: the smallest particle of an element
  • molecule: two or more atoms joined together
  • compound: a substance made of two or more different elements chemically joined
  • chemical formula: symbols and subscripts showing the atoms in a substance, e.g. H₂O
  • extended structure: a large repeating arrangement of atoms, as in a crystal

🔑 Key concepts

  • Subscripts count atoms: CO₂ has 1 C and 2 O.
  • O₂ is a molecule of an element; H₂O is a molecule of a compound.
  • Structure determines properties (diamond is hard, graphite is slippery).
  • Ball-and-stick models represent atoms and bonds.

C4 States of Matter and Particle Motion

Book: Activities 8, 9, 10 (What’s in a State?, Energy and Particle Movement, Modeling State Changes)

💡 Summary

Matter commonly exists as a solid, liquid or gas, and the particle model explains the difference. In a solid, particles are packed close together and vibrate in place; in a liquid, they are close but slide past one another; in a gas, they are far apart and move quickly in all directions. Adding thermal energy makes particles move faster, which can cause melting, evaporation or boiling, while removing energy causes condensation and freezing. During a change of state, the substance stays the same and its mass is conserved; only the arrangement and motion of the particles change.

📝 Key vocabulary

  • solid: definite shape and volume; particles vibrate in fixed positions
  • liquid: definite volume but takes the container’s shape; particles slide past each other
  • gas: no definite shape or volume; particles far apart and fast
  • thermal energy: the energy of moving particles in a substance
  • melting / freezing: solid → liquid / liquid → solid
  • evaporation / condensation: liquid → gas / gas → liquid

🔑 Key concepts

  • Higher temperature = faster particle motion.
  • Changes of state are physical changes.
  • Mass stays the same during a change of state.
  • Gases expand to fill their container and can be compressed.
  • Temperature stays constant during melting or boiling of a pure substance.

C5 Polymers and Plastics

Book: Activities 11, 12, 13 (Making Polymers, Modeling Polymers, The Impact of Plastics on Society)

💡 Summary

A polymer is a very large molecule made of many small repeating units called monomers linked in long chains, like paper clips joined into a chain. Natural polymers include cellulose, starch, proteins and DNA; synthetic polymers include most plastics such as polyethylene. Polymers can be made in the lab, for example by cross-linking glue with borax to make a stretchy slime, and the length and cross-linking of the chains change the material’s flexibility and strength. Plastics are cheap, light and durable, which makes them useful but also means they last for hundreds of years as litter and pollution, so society weighs their benefits against their impacts.

📝 Key vocabulary

  • polymer: a large molecule made of many repeating units joined in a chain
  • monomer: one small repeating unit of a polymer
  • synthetic: made by people rather than found in nature
  • cross-link: a bond that connects two polymer chains
  • biodegradable: able to be broken down by living things such as bacteria

🔑 Key concepts

  • Polymer = many monomers linked together.
  • More cross-links make a polymer stiffer and less stretchy.
  • Plastics are synthetic polymers mostly made from petroleum.
  • Durability is useful but makes plastic waste long-lasting.
  • Reduce, reuse, recycle and biodegradable materials can lessen impacts.
