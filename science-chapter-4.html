🏆 Revision Plan: Unit D

Test coming up? Use this plan the night before, then check the must-know facts and common misconceptions.

⏰ Night-before plan
  1. List the five kinds of evidence of chemical change and give one example of each.
  2. Explain conservation of mass with a closed and an open system.
  3. Count atoms on both sides of two equations.
  4. Define exothermic, endothermic, criteria and constraints.
  5. Take one practice test and review the explanations.

✅ Must-know facts
  • Reactants → products; new substances have new properties.
  • Evidence: gas, color change, precipitate, temperature change, light/odor.
  • Atoms are rearranged, never created or destroyed; mass is conserved in a closed system.
  • Exothermic releases heat (gets warmer); endothermic absorbs heat (gets colder).
  • Batteries change chemical energy into electrical energy.
  • Engineering: criteria vs constraints; prototype → test → refine; change one variable at a time.
  • A more reactive metal displaces copper from solution.

⚠️ Common misconceptions
  • Myth: Mass disappears when something burns or fizzes.
    Actually: Gas escapes; in a closed system mass stays the same.
  • Myth: Any bubbles mean a chemical reaction.
    Actually: Boiling makes bubbles too; that’s physical.
  • Myth: Dissolving is a chemical change.
    Actually: Dissolving is physical; the substance can be recovered.
  • Myth: Cold packs “add cold”.
    Actually: They absorb thermal energy from your skin.
  • Myth: A design either works or fails.
    Actually: Designs are judged against criteria and improved through iteration.

💯 Test tips
  • Ask: did a NEW substance form? That decides chemical vs physical.
  • For mass questions, check whether the system is open or closed.
  • Show your atom counts when explaining balanced equations.
  • Compare designs using the criteria given in the question, one by one.
  • Scenario questions describe a new situation: name the science idea it is really about before you answer.
  • For short answers, write a full sentence that uses the key vocabulary word and gives a reason (“because …”).

D1 Evidence of Chemical Change

Book: Activities 1, 2, 3, 5 (Producing Circuit Boards, Evidence of Chemical Change, Physical Changes and Chemical Reactions, Physical or Chemical Change?)

💡 Summary

In a chemical reaction, starting substances called reactants change into new substances called products with different properties. Evidence that a reaction may have happened includes a gas forming (bubbles), a color change, a precipitate (a solid forming from two liquids), and a temperature change or light. In a physical change, such as melting, dissolving, cutting or bending, the substance stays the same even though its shape or state changes. The unit begins with a real industrial process, making circuit boards, where chemical reactions remove copper and produce waste that must be handled carefully.

📝 Key vocabulary

  • chemical reaction: a process in which substances change into new substances
  • reactant: a starting substance in a reaction
  • product: a new substance formed in a reaction
  • precipitate: a solid that forms when two solutions are mixed
  • physical change: a change in size, shape or state that does not make a new substance

🔑 Key concepts

  • Evidence of chemical change: gas, color change, precipitate, temperature change, light/odor.
  • Evidence suggests a reaction but the real test is whether new substances with new properties formed.
  • Dissolving and changes of state are physical changes.
  • Industrial reactions can create hazardous waste such as copper solutions.

D2 Atoms Rearranging and Conservation of Mass

Book: Activities 4, 6, 7 (Chemical Reactions at the Molecular Scale, Comparing the Masses of Reactants and Products, Explaining Conservation of Mass)

💡 Summary

During a chemical reaction, the atoms in the reactants are rearranged: bonds break and new bonds form, making new molecules, but no atoms are created or destroyed. Because the same atoms are present before and after, the total mass of the products equals the total mass of the reactants; this is the law of conservation of mass. In an open container, a gas product can escape, so the measured mass seems to drop, but in a closed system the mass stays the same. A balanced chemical equation shows the same number of each kind of atom on both sides.

📝 Key vocabulary

  • conservation of mass: in a reaction, the total mass of products equals the total mass of reactants
  • closed system: a system where no matter can enter or leave
  • open system: a system where matter (like gas) can enter or leave
  • chemical equation: symbols and formulas that show reactants → products
  • balanced equation: an equation with equal numbers of each kind of atom on both sides

🔑 Key concepts

  • Atoms are rearranged, not created or destroyed.
  • Mass of reactants = mass of products in a closed system.
  • A gas escaping from an open container makes the measured mass go down.
  • Count each type of atom on both sides to check balance.

D3 Energy in Reactions and Chemical Batteries

Book: Activities 8, 9 (Chemical Batteries, Thermal Energy and Reactions)

💡 Summary

Chemical reactions involve energy as well as matter. Exothermic reactions release thermal energy, so the surroundings get warmer (hand warmers, burning fuel), while endothermic reactions absorb thermal energy, so the surroundings get colder (instant cold packs). A battery uses a chemical reaction between different metals and an electrolyte to push electrons through a circuit, changing chemical energy into electrical energy. Scientists measure temperature change to compare how much energy different reactions release or absorb.

📝 Key vocabulary

  • exothermic: releases thermal energy; the surroundings warm up
  • endothermic: absorbs thermal energy; the surroundings cool down
  • chemical energy: energy stored in the bonds of substances
  • battery: a device that changes chemical energy into electrical energy
  • electrolyte: a solution that conducts electricity and is part of a battery

🔑 Key concepts

  • Exothermic → temperature goes up; endothermic → temperature goes down.
  • A simple battery needs two different metals and an electrolyte.
  • Energy is transformed, not created: chemical → thermal or electrical.
  • Changing amounts of reactants can change how much energy is released.

D4 Engineering a Device with a Reaction

Book: Activities 10, 11 (Developing a Prototype, Refining the Design)

💡 Summary

Engineers use chemical reactions to solve problems, such as designing a portable hand warmer that heats to a safe temperature and stays warm long enough. They begin by defining the criteria (what the design must do) and constraints (limits such as cost, safety and materials). They build a prototype, test it, collect data and then refine the design by changing one variable at a time, a process that repeats until the design meets the criteria. Trade-offs are common: a mixture that gets hotter might not last as long or might cost more.

📝 Key vocabulary

  • criteria: the requirements a design must meet to be successful
  • constraints: limits on a design, such as cost, time, safety or materials
  • prototype: an early model built to test a design
  • iterate: to repeat the cycle of testing and improving
  • variable: a factor that can change in an experiment

🔑 Key concepts

  • Engineering design cycle: define problem → brainstorm → build prototype → test → analyze → refine.
  • Change one variable at a time for a fair test.
  • Compare designs against the criteria using data.
  • Trade-offs mean no design is perfect in every way.

D5 Recovering Copper

Book: Activities 12, 13 (Recovering Copper, Another Approach to Recovering Copper)

💡 Summary

Waste from making circuit boards contains dissolved copper, which is harmful to living things but valuable as a resource. Chemical reactions can recover it: adding a more reactive metal, such as iron or zinc, causes solid copper to come out of the solution while the other metal dissolves. Different recovery methods trade off how much copper they recover, cost, time and the new waste they create. Comparing methods with data and thinking about reusing materials links chemistry to responsible manufacturing.

📝 Key vocabulary

  • recover: to get back a useful material from waste
  • reactive metal: a metal that easily takes part in reactions, such as zinc or iron
  • solution: a mixture in which one substance is dissolved evenly in another
  • hazardous waste: waste that can harm people or the environment
  • recycle: to process used material so it can be used again

🔑 Key concepts

  • Dissolved copper makes solutions blue.
  • A more reactive metal (zinc, iron) can displace copper from solution as solid copper.
  • As copper is removed, the blue color fades.
  • Compare methods by copper recovered, cost, time and leftover waste.
