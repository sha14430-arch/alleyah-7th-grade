10.1 Outcomes and Events

💡 The big idea

An experiment is an activity with results you can watch for, like spinning a spinner or rolling a number cube. Each possible result is an outcome, and an event is a collection of one or more outcomes. When an outcome is part of the event you are looking for, it is called a favorable outcome. Counting outcomes carefully, sometimes with a list, is the first step in finding probabilities.

📝 Key vocabulary

  • experiment: an activity with varying results
  • outcome: one possible result
  • event: a collection of outcomes
  • favorable outcome: an outcome in the event you want

✏️ Worked example

Problem: You roll a number cube (1–6). How many outcomes are in the event "rolling a number greater than 4"?

  1. The favorable outcomes are 5 and 6.

Answer: 2 outcomes

🎯 Practice

  1. How many possible outcomes are there when you spin a spinner with 8 equal sections numbered 1–8?

    Answer: 8

  2. You roll a number cube. List the favorable outcomes for "rolling an even number."

    Answer: 2, 4, 6

  3. A bag has 3 red, 5 blue, and 2 green marbles. How many ways can you choose a marble that is not blue?

    Answer: 5 ways (3 red + 2 green)

  4. You pick a letter from the word MISSISSIPPI. How many favorable outcomes are there for picking an S?

    Answer: 4

  5. You roll a number cube. How many outcomes are in the event "rolling a multiple of 3"?

    Answer: 2 (3 and 6)

10.2 Probability

💡 The big idea

The probability of an event tells how likely it is to happen, using a number from 0 to 1. A probability of 0 means impossible, 1/2 means equally likely to happen or not, and 1 means certain. When all outcomes are equally likely, P(event) = favorable outcomes ÷ total outcomes. You can give a probability as a fraction, a decimal, or a percent.

📝 Key vocabulary

  • probability: a number from 0 to 1 that measures how likely an event is
  • impossible: probability 0
  • certain: probability 1

✏️ Worked example

Problem: A bag has 4 red and 6 blue marbles. What is P(red)?

  1. Favorable outcomes: 4. Possible outcomes: 10.
  2. P(red) = 4/10 = 2/5

Answer: 2/5, or 40%

🎯 Practice

  1. You roll a number cube. What is P(rolling a 5)?

    Answer: 1/6

  2. You roll a number cube. What is P(rolling less than 3)?

    Answer: 1/3

  3. A spinner has 5 equal sections: 2 yellow, 2 green, 1 red. What is P(not red) as a percent?

    Answer: 80%

  4. You roll a number cube. What is P(rolling a 7)? Describe the likelihood.

    Answer: 0 — impossible

  5. A class has 12 boys and 18 girls. A name is drawn at random. What is P(girl) as a decimal?

    Answer: 0.6

10.3 Experimental and Theoretical Probability

💡 The big idea

Theoretical probability is what *should* happen based on the possible outcomes, like 1/2 for heads on a fair coin. Experimental probability is what actually happened when you tried it: (times the event happened) ÷ (total trials). With only a few trials, the two can be quite different, but with many trials, experimental probability usually gets closer to theoretical probability. You can use probability to predict how many times something will happen.

📝 Key vocabulary

  • theoretical probability: probability found by reasoning about outcomes
  • experimental probability: probability found from data in trials
  • trial: one time running the experiment

✏️ Worked example

Problem: You flip a coin 50 times and get 28 heads. Find the experimental and theoretical probabilities of heads.

  1. Experimental: 28/50 = 14/25 = 0.56.
  2. Theoretical: 1/2 = 0.5.

Answer: Experimental 14/25 (0.56); theoretical 1/2 (0.5)

🎯 Practice

  1. A number cube is rolled 60 times and lands on 4 twelve times. Find the experimental probability of getting a 4.

    Answer: 1/5

  2. What is the theoretical probability of rolling a 4 on a number cube?

    Answer: 1/6

  3. A basketball player makes 45 out of 60 free throws. What is the experimental probability she makes the next one?

    Answer: 3/4 (75%)

  4. Using the player above, predict how many of her next 20 free throws she will make.

    Answer: 15

  5. A spinner has 4 equal sections. If you spin it 200 times, about how many times should it land on one particular section?

    Answer: about 50 times

10.4 Compound Events

💡 The big idea

A compound event is made of two or more events, like flipping a coin *and* rolling a number cube. The sample space is the list of all possible outcomes, which you can show with a tree diagram, a table, or an organized list. The Fundamental Counting Principle says that if one event has m outcomes and another has n outcomes, together they have m × n outcomes. Then you can find a probability by counting favorable outcomes in the sample space.

📝 Key vocabulary

  • compound event: an event made of two or more events
  • sample space: the set of all possible outcomes
  • Fundamental Counting Principle: m outcomes and n outcomes give m × n total outcomes

✏️ Worked example

Problem: You flip a coin and roll a number cube. How many outcomes are in the sample space, and what is P(heads and an even number)?

  1. Outcomes: 2 × 6 = 12.
  2. Favorable: H2, H4, H6 → 3.
  3. P = 3/12 = 1/4

Answer: 12 outcomes; 1/4

🎯 Practice

  1. A sandwich shop has 3 breads and 4 fillings. How many different sandwiches with one bread and one filling are possible?

    Answer: 12

  2. You roll two number cubes. How many outcomes are in the sample space?

    Answer: 36

  3. You roll two number cubes. What is P(the sum is 7)?

    Answer: 1/6

  4. You flip 3 coins. What is P(all three are heads)?

    Answer: 1/8

  5. A lock uses 3 digits, each from 0 to 9. How many combinations are possible?

    Answer: 1,000

10.5 Independent and Dependent Events

💡 The big idea

Two events are independent if one happening does not change the probability of the other, like flipping a coin twice. For independent events, P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B). Two events are dependent if the first one changes the chances for the second, like drawing two cards *without putting the first one back*. For dependent events, P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B after A), so you must update the counts after the first pick.

📝 Key vocabulary

  • independent events: events that do not affect each other
  • dependent events: events where one affects the other
  • without replacement: not putting the first item back

✏️ Worked example

Problem: A bag has 3 red and 2 blue marbles. You draw two without replacement. What is P(red, then red)?

  1. First draw: P(red) = 3/5.
  2. Now 2 red out of 4 left: P(red) = 2/4.
  3. P = 3/5 × 2/4 = 6/20 = 3/10

Answer: 3/10

🎯 Practice

  1. You flip a coin and roll a number cube. What is P(tails and a 6)?

    Answer: 1/12

  2. Are the events "draw a card, put it back, draw again" independent or dependent?

    Answer: Independent (putting it back resets the chances)

  3. A bag has 4 green and 6 yellow tiles. You draw one, put it back, and draw again. What is P(green, then green)?

    Answer: 4/25

  4. Same bag, but you do NOT put the first tile back. What is P(green, then green)?

    Answer: 2/15

  5. You spin a spinner with 4 equal colors twice. What is P(blue both times)?

    Answer: 1/16

Extension 10.5 Simulations

💡 The big idea

A simulation is an experiment that models a real situation when the real thing is hard to do. For example, to model whether it rains on each of 3 days with a 50% chance, you could flip 3 coins. You repeat the simulation many times and count how often the event happens to estimate the probability. Random number generators, spinners, and number cubes are common tools for simulations.

📝 Key vocabulary

  • simulation: an experiment that models a real-life situation
  • random number generator: a tool that makes numbers by chance

✏️ Worked example

Problem: A player makes 1/2 of her shots. You flip 2 coins 40 times to model 2 shots; "2 heads" happened 11 times. Estimate the probability she makes both shots.

  1. Experimental probability = 11/40.
  2. 11 ÷ 40 = 0.275

Answer: 11/40, or 27.5%

🎯 Practice

  1. What could you use to simulate an event that has a 1/6 chance of happening?

    Answer: A number cube: let rolling one particular number (like 1) stand for the event.

  2. You use 2 coins to model whether 2 babies are girls. What is the theoretical probability of 2 heads?

    Answer: 1/4

  3. In 50 trials of a simulation, the event happened 18 times. What is the experimental probability as a percent?

    Answer: 36%

  4. A simulation estimates a 30% chance of winning a prize. About how many prizes should you expect in 40 tries?

    Answer: about 12

  5. You roll a number cube to model a 1 in 3 chance: a roll of 1 or 2 means "yes." What is P(yes)?

    Answer: 1/3

10.6 Samples and Populations

💡 The big idea

A population is the entire group you want to know about, like every student in your school. A sample is a smaller part of the population that you actually survey. An unbiased sample represents the whole population fairly, often because it is chosen at random, while a biased sample leaves out or favors certain groups. A good random sample lets you make predictions about the population using proportions.

📝 Key vocabulary

  • population: the whole group being studied
  • sample: a part of the population
  • unbiased sample: a sample that fairly represents the population
  • biased sample: a sample that does not represent the population

✏️ Worked example

Problem: In a random sample of 50 students, 18 prefer tacos. The school has 600 students. Predict how many prefer tacos.

  1. 18/50 = x/600
  2. x = 600 × 18 ÷ 50 = 216

Answer: about 216 students

🎯 Practice

  1. You want to know the favorite sport of 7th graders. Is asking only the basketball team a biased or unbiased sample?

    Answer: Biased (it favors basketball players)

  2. Is choosing 40 student ID numbers at random from the whole school an unbiased sample?

    Answer: Yes, it is random and includes the whole school.

  3. In a random sample of 80 people, 12 are left-handed. Predict how many of 2,000 people are left-handed.

    Answer: 300

  4. In a random sample of 25 light bulbs, 2 are broken. About what percent of all the bulbs are broken?

    Answer: 8%

  5. A sample of 40 students found 30% walk to school. If there are 900 students, about how many walk?

    Answer: about 270

Extension 10.6 Generating Multiple Samples

💡 The big idea

Different random samples from the same population usually give slightly different results. By taking multiple samples, you can see how much the results vary. If the sample results are close together, you can be more confident about your estimate for the population. Finding the mean of several sample results usually gives a better estimate than any one sample.

📝 Key vocabulary

  • variation: how much results differ from each other
  • sample mean: the average of a sample

✏️ Worked example

Problem: Four samples showed 40%, 44%, 38%, and 42% of students like a new lunch item. Estimate the percent for the whole school.

  1. Mean = (40 + 44 + 38 + 42) ÷ 4
  2. = 164 ÷ 4 = 41

Answer: about 41%

🎯 Practice

  1. Three samples gave mean heights of 152 cm, 156 cm, and 154 cm. Estimate the population mean.

    Answer: about 154 cm

  2. Five samples found 6, 8, 7, 5, and 9 red cars out of 20 cars each. What is the mean number of red cars per sample?

    Answer: 7

  3. Using the samples above, estimate the percent of cars that are red.

    Answer: 35%

  4. What is the range of the sample results 6, 8, 7, 5, and 9?

    Answer: 4

  5. Sample set A has results 48, 50, 52 and set B has 30, 50, 70. Which set gives you more confidence in an estimate of 50?

    Answer: Set A (its range is 4, while set B's range is 40)

10.7 Comparing Populations

💡 The big idea

To compare two populations, look at their centers (mean or median) and their variability (spread). The mean absolute deviation (MAD) is the average distance of the data values from the mean, and the interquartile range (IQR) is the spread of the middle half of the data. When the spread is similar, you can describe how far apart the centers are as a multiple of the MAD or IQR. Box plots and dot plots placed side by side make the comparison easy to see.

📝 Key vocabulary

  • measure of center: mean or median
  • mean absolute deviation (MAD): average distance from the mean
  • interquartile range (IQR): third quartile minus first quartile

✏️ Worked example

Problem: Find the mean and MAD of 2, 4, 6, 8, 10.

  1. Mean = 30 ÷ 5 = 6.
  2. Distances from 6: 4, 2, 0, 2, 4. Their sum is 12.
  3. MAD = 12 ÷ 5 = 2.4

Answer: Mean 6, MAD 2.4

🎯 Practice

  1. Find the median of 7, 3, 9, 4, 6.

    Answer: 6

  2. Find the mean of 12, 15, 9, 14.

    Answer: 12.5

  3. Find the MAD of 1, 3, 5, 7.

    Answer: 2

  4. Team A has a mean score of 70 and Team B has a mean of 82. Both have a MAD of 4. How many MADs apart are the means?

    Answer: 3 MADs (82 − 70 = 12, and 12 ÷ 4 = 3)

  5. Data set: 2, 4, 5, 7, 8, 10, 11, 13. Find the IQR (first quartile is the median of the lower half, third quartile is the median of the upper half).

    Answer: 6 (Q1 = 4.5, Q3 = 10.5)
