11.1 Congruent Figures

💡 The big idea

Two figures are congruent if they have exactly the same size and shape, even if one is turned or flipped. In congruent figures, corresponding angles are congruent and corresponding sides are congruent. We write △ABC ≅ △DEF, and the order of the letters tells which parts match: A matches D, B matches E, and C matches F. You can use this to find missing side lengths and angle measures.

📝 Key vocabulary

  • congruent figures: figures with the same size and shape
  • corresponding sides/angles: matching parts of two figures
  • ≅: the symbol for "is congruent to"

✏️ Worked example

Problem: △ABC ≅ △DEF, AB = 7 cm, and ∠B = 50°. Find DE and ∠E.

  1. A ↔ D and B ↔ E, so side AB matches side DE.
  2. DE = 7 cm, and ∠E matches ∠B, so ∠E = 50°.

Answer: DE = 7 cm, ∠E = 50°

    11.2 Translations

    💡 The big idea

    A translation moves every point of a shape the same amount in the same direction, like sliding a book across a desk. The new figure is called the image, and we label it with primes, like A′ (read "A prime"). In the coordinate plane, a translation adds the same numbers to every point: (x, y) → (x + a, y + b). A translated figure is congruent to the original and faces the same way.

    📝 Key vocabulary

    • transformation: a change in a figure's position, size, or orientation
    • translation: a slide
    • image: the figure after a transformation
    • prime (′): the mark used to name image points

    ✏️ Worked example

    Problem: Translate A(2, −1) 4 units left and 3 units up.

    1. Left 4 means x − 4: 2 − 4 = −2.
    2. Up 3 means y + 3: −1 + 3 = 2.

    Answer: A′(−2, 2)

      11.3 Reflections

      💡 The big idea

      A reflection flips a figure over a line called the line of reflection, making a mirror image. Each image point is the same distance from the line as the original point, but on the other side. Reflecting in the x-axis changes the sign of the y-coordinate: (x, y) → (x, −y). Reflecting in the y-axis changes the sign of the x-coordinate: (x, y) → (−x, y).

      📝 Key vocabulary

      • reflection: a flip over a line
      • line of reflection: the line a figure is flipped over
      • mirror image: a reflected copy facing the opposite way

      ✏️ Worked example

      Problem: Reflect B(3, −5) in the x-axis and in the y-axis.

      1. x-axis: keep x, change the sign of y → (3, 5).
      2. y-axis: change the sign of x, keep y → (−3, −5).

      Answer: x-axis: (3, 5); y-axis: (−3, −5)

        11.4 Rotations

        💡 The big idea

        A rotation turns a figure around a fixed point called the center of rotation. The angle of rotation tells how far it turns, and the direction is clockwise or counterclockwise. Turning about the origin follows patterns: 90° counterclockwise sends (x, y) → (−y, x), 180° sends (x, y) → (−x, −y), and 90° clockwise sends (x, y) → (y, −x). Rotated figures are congruent to the original.

        📝 Key vocabulary

        • rotation: a turn around a point
        • center of rotation: the point the figure turns around
        • angle of rotation: how many degrees the figure turns

        ✏️ Worked example

        Problem: Rotate C(4, 1) 90° counterclockwise about the origin.

        1. Rule: (x, y) → (−y, x).
        2. (4, 1) → (−1, 4)

        Answer: C′(−1, 4)

          11.5 Similar Figures

          💡 The big idea

          Two figures are similar if they have the same shape, though they can be different sizes. In similar figures, corresponding angles are congruent and corresponding side lengths are proportional, which means they all have the same ratio. We write △ABC ∼ △DEF. You can set up a proportion with corresponding sides to find a missing length.

          📝 Key vocabulary

          • similar figures: same shape, sides in proportion
          • ∼: the symbol for "is similar to"
          • proportional: having equal ratios

          ✏️ Worked example

          Problem: Rectangle A is 4 cm by 6 cm. Rectangle B is similar, with a short side of 10 cm. Find its long side.

          1. 4/6 = 10/x
          2. 4x = 60, so x = 15

          Answer: 15 cm

            11.6 Perimeters and Areas of Similar Figures

            💡 The big idea

            For similar figures, the number you multiply each side length by is the scale factor, k. The ratio of their perimeters is the same as the scale factor, k. But the ratio of their areas is the square of the scale factor, k². So if you double every side of a shape, the perimeter doubles but the area becomes 4 times as big.

            📝 Key vocabulary

            • scale factor: the ratio of corresponding side lengths
            • ratio of perimeters: equals the scale factor
            • ratio of areas: equals the scale factor squared

            ✏️ Worked example

            Problem: Two similar triangles have a scale factor of 3. The small one has perimeter 12 cm and area 6 cm². Find the large one's perimeter and area.

            1. Perimeter × 3: 12 × 3 = 36 cm.
            2. Area × 3² = × 9: 6 × 9 = 54 cm².

            Answer: 36 cm and 54 cm²

              11.7 Dilations

              💡 The big idea

              A dilation makes a figure bigger or smaller by multiplying distances from a center point by a scale factor k. When the center is the origin, you multiply each coordinate by k: (x, y) → (kx, ky). If k > 1 the dilation is an enlargement, and if 0 < k < 1 it is a reduction. A dilated figure is similar to the original, not congruent (unless k = 1).

              📝 Key vocabulary

              • dilation: a transformation that resizes a figure
              • center of dilation: the fixed point the figure grows or shrinks from
              • enlargement / reduction: k > 1 / 0 < k < 1

              ✏️ Worked example

              Problem: Dilate the triangle A(1, 2), B(3, 0), C(0, −1) with scale factor 3 (center at the origin).

              1. Multiply each coordinate by 3.
              2. A′(3, 6), B′(9, 0), C′(0, −3)

              Answer: A′(3, 6), B′(9, 0), C′(0, −3); it is an enlargement.
